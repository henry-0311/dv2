Doing more with less

Australian farms now produce almost twice as much from the same land, labour, machinery and materials as they did in the late 1970s. Here is which farms got there, how, and where, and why it matters for the food Malaysia buys from Australia.

1What is farm productivity?

Productivity compares what farms produce (grain, meat, wool and milk) with what they use to produce it: land, labour, machinery, and materials such as fuel and fertiliser. When output grows faster than inputs, farms are becoming more productive.

The figures come from ABARES, the Australian Government's farm research bureau. It tracks broadacre farms, the large farms that grow grain or graze sheep and cattle over wide areas, and dairy farms. Each figure is an index: the starting year equals 100, so 150 means one and a half times as much output from the same inputs.

2Which industries pulled ahead?

Not every farm improved at the same pace. Cropping farms, which grow wheat, barley and canola, now get 2.8 times as much from the same inputs as in 1977–⁠78. Sheep farms ended 2024–⁠25 at 101, almost exactly where they started.

3Where does the growth come from?

There are two ways to raise productivity: produce more, or use less. Cropping farms did the first. Their output grew 2.9% a year, faster than their inputs (1.4% a year).

Sheep farms did the second. They produced less each year (−1.9%), but cut their inputs even faster (−2.3%), so they still gained 0.4% a year. Mixed farms followed the same path as sheep farms.

From output to productivity: average change per year, 1977–⁠78 to 2024–⁠25

Read each panel from left to right. Output: how fast output grew. Inputs: the effect of inputs. Using more inputs pulls productivity down and using fewer pushes it up, so this bar is input growth with the sign flipped. Result: productivity growth.

Source: ABARES (2026), tables 7 (productivity), 8 (inputs) and 9 (output), all of Australia. The result can differ from the first two steps by 0.1 because ABARES rounds each rate separately.

Across all broadacre farms, labour fell 2.0% a year and capital 1.2%, while materials such as fuel, fertiliser and chemicals rose 1.8% a year. In short, farms replaced workers with purchased materials.

Cropping is the exception that explains its lead: it used more of almost everything, especially materials (+4.0% a year) and land (+1.1%), and turned them into far more grain.

4Where in Australia?

Productivity grew in every state. The Northern Territory (1.5% a year) and Western Australia (1.4%) grew fastest, and Tasmania was slowest (0.4%).

Use the drop-down above the map to compare industries. Sheep farms in Western Australia, for example, grew 2.6% a year, while those in New South Wales went backwards (−0.4%).

States took different roads. The Northern Territory and Western Australia mostly produced more: output rose 1.9% and 1.2% a year. Tasmania went the other way. Its output fell 1.5% a year, but it cut inputs even faster (−1.9%).

Which state has improved most?

Every state starts at 100, so a rank here shows growth since 1977–⁠78, not which state has the best farms. South Australia led for four decades. Western Australia moved to first in 2020–⁠24.

The Northern Territory climbed from 7th to 4th, then slipped to 6th. Its fast growth rate partly reflects a weak start: its index averaged 88 in 1977–⁠89, below its starting level.

5Take the weather out

Australia's weather swings widely, so one good or bad season can hide the real trend. ABARES also estimates productivity with the effects of weather and climate removed, using a machine-learning model. Its estimates start in 1988–⁠89.

With the weather taken out, the line is much smoother and the dips in harsh seasons become much smaller. Broadacre productivity still rose about 35% between 1988–⁠89 and 2024–⁠25.

By state, Western Australia stands out: +71% with the weather removed. New South Wales and Queensland gained about 15%.

The Northern Territory (+5%) and Tasmania (+4%) barely moved. So the Territory's fast growth since 1977–⁠78 seems to owe more to its weak start and good seasons than to steady gains since 1988–⁠89.

6From Australian farms to Malaysian tables

Australia sends over 70% of the value of its farm production overseas, so these gains reach well beyond Australia. In 2023–⁠24 Malaysia bought A$1.8 billion (1.8 billion Australian dollars) of Australian farm products, making it Australia's 10th-largest farm market. China was the largest, at A$16.2 billion.

Every gain in productivity means more grain, meat and milk from the same land and labour, and much of it is shipped to Asia.

What to remember

Almost twice as much

Broadacre farms now produce 1.9 times as much from the same inputs as in 1977–⁠78, even though harsh seasons still knock them back.

Cropping led, sheep stalled

Cropping farms are 2.8 times as productive as in 1977–⁠78. Sheep farms are back where they started.

Fewer workers, more materials

Labour use fell 2.0% a year while materials such as fuel and fertiliser rose 1.8% a year.
